The spark was the 2025 Monaco Grand Prix. The podium: Norris, Leclerc, Piastri — in exactly the order they qualified. Behind them, a good chunk of the field finished a lap down, unable to pass anyone. Nothing unusual for Monaco, long known as the hardest circuit on the calendar to overtake on — but that day it crystallized a question I'd been sitting with as a fan for a while: is this sense of predictability just a Monaco thing, or a real underlying trend in F1?

Since the 2017 regulation changes — wider, heavier cars with more complex aerodynamics — the idea that F1 has become more predictable keeps coming back among fans and commentators. I wanted to test that impression against data rather than take it at face value: does qualifying position really determine the race result more and more?

I built a pipeline that pulls real race, qualifying, and sprint results through the Jolpica-F1 API (the community successor to the old Ergast API, which shut down in 2024), for the 2023, 2024, and 2025 seasons — 70 races, 1,397 individual results. For each season, I compute the correlation between qualifying position and final position, along with the pole-to-win conversion rate.

The result doesn't match a story of steady decline. The qualifying-to-result correlation averages 0.667 across the three seasons — well below the 0.80 threshold I treat as a genuine predictability "crisis." And it doesn't move in a straight line either: 0.60 in 2023, a peak at 0.75 in 2024, then back down to 0.65 in 2025. The win-from-pole rate sits at 61% — meaningful, but far from automatic.

The data doesn't support the idea of an F1 that's mechanically becoming more of a foregone conclusion. It's worth watching season over season, especially since the 2026 regulations were explicitly designed to address this perception — the dashboard stays wired to real data to track how it evolves.
