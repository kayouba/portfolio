The "best third-place teams" rule doesn't sit well with a lot of football fans — many see it as a system that bails teams out rather than rewarding them, diluting the cost of a bad group stage. I didn't have a strong opinion on the debate, just genuine curiosity: with 48 teams, what does this mechanism actually change for a team on the pitch?

Starting in 2026, the World Cup expands from 32 to 48 teams across 12 groups of 4: the top two in each group qualify directly, and the 8 best third-place teams out of 12 fill out the round of 32. FIFA's rulebook sets the tiebreak criteria — points, goal difference, goals scored — but never states what points total actually gives a team good odds of advancing. It's a concrete question any coach would ask, and nobody had put a number on it.

I simulated 50,000 complete group stages using a deliberately neutral match model: each game has roughly a 38% win chance for either side and a 24% draw chance, calibrated on historical World Cup frequencies rather than a FIFA ranking. The goal wasn't to predict which teams will qualify, but to measure, for a given points total, the probability of advancing — independent of team strength.

The thresholds that come out are sharp. With 2 points (two draws and a loss), the probability of advancing drops to 2%. With 3 points (one win, two losses), it already climbs to 57%. And from 4 points onward (one win, one draw), it reaches 99%. In other words: across three group matches, a single win — even paired with two losses — is, statistically, enough half the time to reach the round of 16.

The 48-team format is debated: it opens the tournament to more nations, but also dilutes the cost of a bad group-stage match. This project doesn't take a side in that debate — it just hands a concrete number to whoever is having it: under this format, a forgiving group stage can be enough to qualify without dominating your group.
